# OLE HOVEDSKOV — Character Development, pass 1

Twenty years old. A violinist. The one the record forgot, which makes him the one we get to build.

Everything under **KNOWN** comes from the sources in the folder. Everything under **PROPOSED** is invented and can be thrown out.

## 2. THE CORE (proposed)

**What he wants:** to play. Specifically, to get out of Randers and into a real orchestra. He has a date in his head: the Radio Symphony auditions, spring 1944. Every choice he makes in November 1943 is made by someone who believes he has a spring.

**What he needs:** to find out whether he is anything when the violin is taken away. The film answers: yes. But it costs him everything to learn it.

**The wound:** he is thirteen years younger than his brother. He grew up in Børge's shadow, the family's second artist, the one who was "also musical". He has never once been the brave one. Langå is the first thing he ever did that Børge didn't do first. (Note: whether Børge was on the bridge is disputed. This document assumes he was NOT on the bridge but was in the network. See §7.)

**The lie he believes:** that his hands are who he is.

**Theme fit:** Stella's line, "makers who became breakers", lives most literally in him. The others break the bridge and are broken quickly, by a bullet. Ole is broken slowly, over eight years, and the thing that breaks is the exact thing that made him: his lungs, his breath, his hands. He is the film's long note.

## 3. THE TRADE (proposed)

**Training:** Det Jyske Musikkonservatorium, Aarhus (founded 1927, so this is plausible). Enrolled 1940, the autumn the Germans came. Commutes or lodges. This puts him on the Aarhus–Randers train line weekly. He knows the Langå bridges the way a commuter knows them: the change in sound as the wheels go from embankment to iron and back.

**Money:** "professional" at twenty in 1943 means dance orchestras, restaurant trios, the odd church wedding, playing for the cinema when the sound system fails. Randers had a theatre, cafés, and hotels, and hotels had German officers. Which raises the question in §7.

**The instrument:** not a great one. A German trade violin, Markneukirchen, bought second-hand by his father when Ole was eleven. He knows exactly what it is and exactly what it isn't. He has promised himself something better after the Radio audition. The irony that his violin is German is not lost on him and he is tired of the joke.

**The ear:** absolute pitch. Proposed as his defining trait because it gives us a visual and audible language for him that nobody else in the film has. He hears rooms. He knows a train's speed by its pitch on the rails. When the sources say "one of the policemen whistled *Det var en lørdag aften* as the all-clear", our version is that it is Ole who identifies it: not just the tune, but that it is whistled a quarter-tone flat, the way that particular constable always whistles it, and therefore it is him and not a German who has learned the signal. The musician's ear saves the mission. (INVENTED, and I think it should stay.)

**The hands and the explosive:** Nobel 808 was the explosive at Langå. It is plastic, kneaded by hand, smells of almonds, and the nitroglycerin absorbed through the skin gives everyone who handles it a splitting headache within the hour. The "808 headache" is real and every saboteur of the period mentions it. So: a violinist kneading explosive into shape with the fingers he has spent nine years training, then riding home with his skull splitting, then washing the almond smell off before his mother wakes. That is the scene.

## 4. THE FAMILY (proposed detail on known people)

**Peter Valdemar, the father.** Gas and water master for Randers: a municipal man, a keeper of pipes and pressure, a man whose whole life is about things flowing safely under the ground. Two sons who both became artists. He does not understand either of them and has paid for both. He pays for the violin. He will pay for the sanatorium. He never once says he is proud, and then, at the end, does something that says it.

**Wilhelmine, the mother.** The one who noticed the almond smell. She is the household's intelligence service. She works out what Ole is doing at least a week before the bridge, and says nothing, because saying something would make her responsible for stopping him and she cannot decide whether she wants to.

**Børge, the brother.** Thirty-three. Copenhagen Academy, married to a painter, already a name in a small way. The resistance came to him first, through the art world, and he brought Ole in, or Ole followed him in; either way Børge is the reason. He is out of the country before Ole is arrested. He spends the rest of his life making monuments to the people he left behind, and one of them is his brother. The two of them have one scene together after the war, at Vejlefjord, and it must be the best-written scene in the film.

## 5. THE GIRL (proposed)

The notes are right that there was someone. Three candidates. **DECIDED (5 Sep 2026): Option A, Inger. Ole and Inger are a duo playing the dining room at Hotel Randers with German officers present. See SCENES.md scenes 5, 6, 17, 38.**

**A. INGER, the accompanist.** Piano student at the same conservatory, a year older, from a farm outside Randers. They play together for money: weddings, the Hotel Randers dining room, Saturday dances. Music gives them scenes with no dialogue, which is what the film needs from them. She is the one waiting on the platform at the end of the white-bus journey, and the one who, in 1951, is asked by the family what to do with the violin.

**B. A nurse at Randers Hospital.** Ties her to Røjel's world and gives the film a face inside the medical system for the sanatorium years. Weaker: too convenient.

**C. A KU girl, the political one.** She is the reason he joined. Interesting for the argument scenes, but it makes his choice about her, and it should be about him.

Whichever she is, the rule: she does not die, she does not betray, she does not become a symbol. She is the person who has to live the six years of slow goodbye, and after 1951 she goes on. The cyclist in the framing device may be her grandchild. (Hold that thought; don't decide yet.)

## 6. THE ARC — seven movements

**I. THE SPRING HE THINKS HE HAS (Sept–Oct 1943)**
Randers after 29 August: the Germans have taken over, the army is disarmed, the mood has hardened. Ole is practising the Sibelius for the Radio audition. Sven Johannesen, whom he knows slightly from KU dances, asks him a question that isn't quite a question. He says yes before Sven has finished asking. He tells himself it's for Denmark. It's partly to have something Børge doesn't.

**II. THE NIGHT (17–18 Nov 1943)**
Six bicycles in the sleet. Sacks of almond-smelling clay. The shot from the darkness and thirty seconds of believing it's over. The whistled tune, a quarter-tone flat. Kneading the charge onto a bearing in the freezing dark with hands he can no longer feel. Pressing the time pencil. Riding home with a headache like a spike. Bed at three. Nothing. Nothing. Then at 5.25 a sound from the south that he feels in the bedframe before he hears it. He lies still and counts the intervals: 6.15, 7.35, 9.41. Four. He knows what six would have sounded like.

**III. THIRTEEN DAYS (18–30 Nov)**
He plays a dance on the Saturday. Otto and Anders are already taken. Sven is taken on the Friday, Oluf that evening. He learns it from a newspaper boy. Røjel is gone. Hoff stays and Ole stays because Hoff stays, and because leaving would mean telling his father why. He plays a wedding on the 27th. The bride's uncle is a policeman. On the 30th they come for Hoff in the morning and for Ole in the afternoon. He is practising. He asks if he can put the violin in its case first, and they let him.

**IV. THE MACHINE (Dec 1943 – May 1944)**
Aarhus, then Copenhagen. He learns of Hoff's death from a guard who thinks he'll enjoy it. He learns of Skæring from the silence. Interrogation: they have Sven's confession, Oluf's confession, everything, so there is nothing to protect and he tells them nothing anyway, out of a stubbornness he didn't know he had. Court-martial, 16 May: death. Torstensson beside him. He is twenty.

**V. THE CELL (May–Sept 1944)**
Vestre Fængsel. The Hvidsten men are in the same wing. On 29 June, eight of them are taken out at dawn. He hears it. Then the executions stop, and no one tells him why, and he spends the summer waiting for a dawn that doesn't come. He has no instrument. He plays the Sibelius on his forearm with his left hand and hears every note. On 19 September a piece of paper says "livsvarigt". He is going to Germany. He is going to live.

**VI. GERMANY (Oct 1944 – April 1945)**
Dreibergen-Bützow. Cold, hunger, a cough that starts in January and doesn't stop. He stops playing on his forearm because his fingers won't close. In April a Swedish bus with a red cross painted on the roof. He does not believe it until Malmö.

**VII. THE LONG NOTE (1945–1951)**
Home. The platform. Inger. His mother. His father, who has brought the violin, and Ole who cannot lift his arm to take it. Vejlefjord: a bed on a veranda, sea air, the disease advancing and retreating. Børge comes, once, from Gothenburg, with drawings for a monument. The brothers' scene. Ole in 1951: twenty-eight, and the war has been over for six years, and he is the last of the six to die of it. Buried under Kaj Munk's verse with the men who were shot in 1943, eight years late.

## 7. OPEN DECISIONS (for Frank, Stella, Pete)

1. **Did he play for Germans?** Randers hotels hired musicians; the Wehrmacht had officers' evenings. If Ole took that money in 1942, we get the Birk-father theme inside the hero: the compromise before the resistance. It also gives him a private reason for Langå. Recommend YES, once, and he never forgives himself.

2. **Was Børge on the bridge?** Straarup's list of six is firm and Børge isn't on it. Recommend: Børge is the recruiter, not a saboteur, and is in Sweden by December. That makes his guilt the engine of the brothers' scene.

3. **Which girl?** Recommend Inger (A). Decide before we build the framing device, because the cyclist may be hers.

4. **The violin's fate.** Options: buried with him; given to a pupil; Inger keeps it and never plays it; sold by the father to pay the sanatorium. The last is the cruellest and probably the truest.

5. **His voice.** Recommend: quiet, exact, listens more than he talks, describes things in terms of sound. Never makes a speech. The others get the speeches. He gets one line at the court-martial, and it's about music, and the judges don't understand it.

6. **Does he know why the executions stopped?** Historically the halt followed Hvidsten and the Copenhagen general strike. If Ole never learns that eight innkeepers' deaths bought his life, the audience carries that for him. Recommend he never knows.

## 8. MOTIFS TO CARRY THROUGH THE FILM

- **Pitch.** Train on iron, train on embankment. Time pencils. The whistle. The bedframe at 5.25. His forearm in the cell. The cough, which has a pitch too.
- **Almonds.** The 808 smell in November. Marzipan at Christmas 1943 in a cell. The smell in the sanatorium kitchen in 1950 that makes him sit up in bed.
- **Hands.** Nine years of practice. One night of clay. Six months of cold. Six years of watching them thin.
- **The German violin.** Bought by a Danish father, played for Danish weddings and possibly German officers, silent through a German prison, brought to a Danish platform by the father who bought it.
